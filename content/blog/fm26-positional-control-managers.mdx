Five managers on this site are usually filed under the same word: control. Arteta, Alonso, Nagelsmann, Flick, Emery. It is a useful shorthand and a misleading one, because "control" describes where a team ends up, not how it gets there.

Take two examples from that list. Arteta's Arsenal and Flick's Barcelona both want the ball, both push players into the final third, both defend with a line that lives near the halfway line. Put their tactics boards side by side and the differences are subtle. Watch twenty minutes of each and they are different sports: one removes the press before it arrives, the other invites it and steps the line as the answer.

This article sorts the five by mechanism rather than by manager. If you are choosing a system for a save, the question is not who you like — it is which lever your squad can actually pull.

## The four levers

Ignore formations for a moment. Every control system makes four decisions:

**Where do you win the ball back?** Arteta and Guardiola press to protect their shape; Nagelsmann, Flick and Emery press to attack the turnover.

**How do you build out?** Either you create a spare man behind the ball, or you deliberately leave a man free in front of it and dare the opponent to step up.

**Where does your width come from?** Wide forwards, attacking full-backs, or wing-backs. This is the single most common reason a copied system looks nothing like the original.

**What do you ask of your last line?** This is the expensive one. Some systems need defenders who can defend in a large space. Others need defenders who can pass. Very few squads have both.

| | Arteta | Alonso | Nagelsmann | Flick | Emery |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base shape | 4-3-3 | 3-4-2-1 | 4-2-3-1 | 4-2-3-1 | 4-4-2 |
| In possession | 2-3-5 | 3-box-3 | 3-2-5 | 3-3-4 | 4-2-2-2 |
| Width from | Right-back | Wing-backs | Both full-backs | Both full-backs | Wide midfielders |
| Press height | Middle third | Middle third | Opposition half | Opposition half | Middle third |
| Main risk | Slow build-up | Wing-back channel | Defence behind the line | Defence behind the line | Middle four stretched |
| Defender demand | Passing | Passing + pace | Pace + decisions | Pace + decisions | Aerial + discipline |

The table is the summary. The rest of this article is the reasoning, system by system.

## Arteta: removing the press instead of beating it

Arteta's solution to being pressed is to make the press pointless. One full-back steps into midfield, the ball moves through a three-man midfield at walking pace, and there is nothing to press because every player who receives the ball has two short options and no reason to hurry.

In FM26 this is the safest of the five systems to set up, because the risk is loaded onto a player you can replace. If your inverted full-back is having a bad game, substitute him. If your whole defensive line is having a bad game — which is what Flick and Nagelsmann ask for — you lose 3-0.

Read the shape as three layers. The back two are centre-backs, and they stay centre-backs — that is the whole point. The midfield three is the inverted full-back, the pivot, and a box-to-box runner. The front five is the attacking right-back, both wide forwards, the advanced playmaker arriving between the lines, and the striker.

The settings that make it work:

- **In possession:** Fairly Narrow width, Shorter passing, Standard tempo, Play Out of Defence, Work Ball Into Box, focus Through The Middle.
- **In transition:** Counter on, Counter-Press on, Roll It Out. The counter-press exists to recover the ball for another build-up, not to launch one.
- **Out of possession:** Higher line of engagement, Higher defensive line, More Often trigger press, Prevent Short GK Distribution.

Where it goes wrong: the pivot. If your deep-lying playmaker is slow to turn, the whole thing becomes sideways passing with a right-back stranded upfield. Arteta's system is also the one that suffers most when the opposition simply refuses to engage — you can hold 70% of the ball against a 5-4-1 and create nothing, which is the single most common complaint in the FM26 community.

## Alonso: three centre-backs and two number 10s

Alonso's system is the most structurally generous of the five. A back three means somebody is always spare in build-up, which is why Leverkusen could play out from the back against anyone.

The price is paid further forward, in a specific and awkward place: the wing-back channel. Alonso's wing-backs are the team's entire width budget, so when the ball is lost they are 40 metres from where they need to be. Every goal conceded by a naive copy of this tactic comes down the same corridor.

Two asymmetries matter. The left wing-back attacks and the right one supports; if both bomb forward you have a back three defending a five-man width without the five. And the midfield pair splits: one screens, one dictates. Alonso's Leverkusen were not two destroyers — they were a screener and a passer, with the passing partner trusted to break lines.

Then the two 10s, the part everyone copies badly. In FM26 they are positioned as inside forwards but they are not wingers. Their instruction set should include Roam From Position, and their job is to be where the opposition's midfield cannot follow: one drops to receive, the other runs beyond. If both drift wide, the wing-backs have no lane and the striker is isolated between two centre-backs.

Setup notes: Fairly Wide width, Mixed passing, Higher tempo, Play Out of Defence, Pass Into Space, Overlap Both Sides, Counter and Counter-Press on, Higher engagement line, Higher defensive line, More Often trigger press, narrow defensive width.

## Nagelsmann: cutting lanes, not chasing the ball

Here is where the five systems stop being variations on a theme. Klopp's press chases the ball. Nagelsmann's press removes the pass.

The front four step into the lanes the opponent wants to use, the defensive line follows them up, and the opponent is left with three choices: a long ball into a space where your centre-backs are waiting, a pass that does not exist, or a mistake. It is the most aggressive defensive idea in this article after Flick's, and it is the one that most often produces the "why is my team 3-0 down after 20 minutes" post.

The settings that define the system are only two: Line of Engagement Much Higher and Defensive Line Much Higher. Everything else — both full-backs on Attack, the pivot screening rather than pressing, Trigger Press More Often — is there to support those two lines.

What kills it: a centre-back who does not step up. In a normal tactic a hesitant defender costs you a goal occasionally. In this one he breaks the trap, and the space behind an already-high line becomes a one-on-one. Check Positioning and Decisions before you commit, and expect to need two or three matches of familiarity before the line holds.

## Flick: the same shape, the opposite risk

Flick's board is close to Nagelsmann's. Same 4-2-3-1, same attacking full-backs, same inside forwards, same demand for pace at centre-back. If you skim the two setups you could conclude they are the same tactic with a different name.

They are not, and the difference is what the defensive line is for.

Nagelsmann's line exists to support the press. Flick's line *is* the defensive plan: the offside trap is switched on as a standing instruction, and the team's plan for the opponent's counter-attack is to have already stepped up past the striker before the pass is made. That is a genuinely different risk profile. Nagelsmann's system drops to a Higher line when the press fails. Flick's has to be switched off manually, and the games where you forget are the ones that cost you the title.

Four settings define it: Defensive Line Much Higher, Offside Trap on, Trigger Press Much More Often, Prevent Short GK Distribution on. Add Force Inside as defensive width if you want the trap to have a direction — pushing the opponent wide and then stepping is easier to coach than trapping centrally.

The honest warning: this is the system most likely to produce a 4-3 win and a 0-3 defeat in the same month. It is for squads that are already among the best in their league, where the opponent spends most of the match defending and rarely gets a clean run at your line.

## Emery: the 4-4-2 that presses

Emery is in this article for a different reason than the other four. His system does not try to dominate the ball for its own sake; it uses a compact midfield to force the opponent into a trap, then attacks the space that opens in about three passes.

The 4-4-2 is the shell. Two banks of four, narrow, with the front two curving their runs to push the ball towards a touchline. When the pass goes wide, the ball-side midfielder and full-back close it, and the turnover happens in a place where the opponent's full-back is already committed upfield.

Emery's system is the most flexible of the five for a mid-table save, and the reason is boring: it needs less. No ball-playing centre-backs, no inverted full-back, no elite pivot. It needs wide players with stamina, two central midfielders who can run, and a striker who can hold the ball while everyone else catches up.

What it struggles with: elite possession sides. A 4-4-2 press is a shape, and shapes can be played around by teams with three technical midfielders who move the ball faster than your two banks can shift. When that happens, do not raise the press line further — drop it, stay compact, and take the game to the wings on the counter.

## Picking one

**Squad is better than the league, defenders can pass, you enjoy watching the ball move:** Arteta or Alonso. Alonso if you want the two 10s, Arteta if you want the safer shape.

**Squad is better than the league, defenders are fast rather than technical:** Nagelsmann or Flick. Flick if your centre-backs are the best in the division; Nagelsmann if you want a system you can dial down when the press fails.

**Mid-table or lower, and you need something that works with the players you have:** Emery. It is the only one of the five that does not require a transfer window first.

**Promoted side with a slow defence:** none of them. Take the low-block systems instead, which is what the [pragmatic school comparison](/blog/fm26-low-block-counter-managers) covers.

## Matching the system to the opponent

Squad fit gets you into the season. Matchup fit is what decides the games where two good teams meet, and the five systems are not equally good against the same opponents. This is the part that single-club guides never cover, because they only ever describe one system.

| Opponent | Best of the five | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A high-pressing side | Arteta | The inverted full-back gives a free receiver against a press that commits players forward |
| A deep low block | Alonso or Emery | Both can attack with genuine width — wing-backs or wide midfielders — instead of asking a narrow shape to break a narrow defence |
| A direct long-ball team | Emery | Two banks of four in the middle third are the natural counter to a team trying to skip midfield |
| An elite possession side | Ancelotti's school | Conceding the ball on purpose removes their main weapon; see the [pragmatic systems](/blog/fm26-low-block-counter-managers) |
| A fast counter-attacking side | Arteta or Alonso | Both keep three or more players behind the ball at all times |
| A team that man-marks your pivot | Alonso | Three centre-backs and two 10s give you spare receivers no marking scheme can cover |

Two specific traps worth naming.

**Do not use Flick's or Nagelsmann's system against a fast, direct opponent.** Both defend with a high line, and a team whose plan is a long ball into the channel behind that line has exactly the weapon you are least equipped to survive. That is a matchup problem, not a tactical failure — switch to a lower line for one match rather than rebuilding the tactic.

**Do not use Arteta's or Flick's system against a team that refuses to press.** The first pass of a control system is an invitation, and a passive opponent declines it. Both systems then need manual intervention: raise the width, raise the tempo, and push one player into the block with individual instructions. Alonso's system suffers less because a back three can carry the ball forward, and Emery's suffers least because direct football does not care whether the opponent presses.

## What breaks all five

| Failure | What you will see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The opponent never presses | 65% possession, no chances, 0-0 or a 1-0 defeat to a set piece | Raise width, raise tempo one notch, push one full-back to Attack |
| Pressure without penetration | Passes in front of the block, no passes between the lines | Set the advanced playmaker to Roam, check the striker is dropping |
| The line steps, the offside fails | Repeated one-on-ones against your keeper | Check Positioning and Decisions, drop one line, or switch the trap off |
| Tactical familiarity not built | Everything works for 30 minutes, then the shape falls apart | Play three friendlies, do not judge the system in competitive matches before that |
| Fatigue after 70 minutes | Press stops, the block stretches, late goals conceded | Squad rotation, or accept the last 20 minutes are a different game |
